Operational Pain in Banking Production, SRE and Post-Production Support

A research study of production, SRE and post-production challenges in banking and financial services

July 2026

Evidence base primarily 2025–2026. Prepared for venture diligence and CIO validation.

How to read this report

This report answers a single question: what actually hurts in banking after software reaches production, who feels it, what it costs, and where the technology investment is going. It is not a description of SRE practice in general.

On sources. Analyst subscription research (Gartner, Forrester, IDC) is largely paywalled. Where an analyst figure appears here it comes from a public press release, a vendor-commissioned summary, or a secondary citation, and it is labelled as such. Nothing in this report is an invented citation.

Every material claim carries one of three labels:

  • [primary] – regulatory, supervisory or parliamentary evidence. These carry the argument.
  • [vendor] – vendor or vendor-sponsored survey. Directionally useful, discount accordingly.
  • [secondary] – widely cited but not verified at original source.

Unmarked statements are the author’s analysis. Regional judgements that are inference rather than evidence are marked (inferred).

Evidence density is uneven by region. Europe, the UK and the US are supported by supervisory and parliamentary data. India, Nigeria and Brazil rest on regulator statements and credible national press. The Middle East, the rest of Africa, the rest of Latin America and much of Asia-Pacific rest largely on analyst and vendor material, because no equivalent public incident disclosure regime exists. Do not present the thin regions with the same confidence as the thick ones.

Terminology warning – two things called DORA

This report uses the acronym in two distinct senses, and a banking audience will conflate them.

  • DORA (regulation) – the EU Digital Operational Resilience Act. Governs ICT risk, incident reporting, resilience testing and third-party oversight.
  • DORA (metrics) – DevOps Research and Assessment, Google’s software delivery research. Produces deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and failed deployment recovery time.

Where ambiguity is possible below, the sense is stated explicitly. This is worth calling out in any presentation to a mixed audience of engineering and risk.

Executive summary

Six conclusions carry the rest of the report.

1. Incidents are a permanent operating condition, not an exception. EU financial entities reported 3,383 major ICT incidents in 2025, an average of 282 a month [primary]. Nine UK banks accumulated 803 hours of unplanned outage across 158 separate failures in two years [primary]. The industry question is no longer prevention. It is containment speed and evidence quality.

2. The dominant cause is ordinary change and process, not attackers. Around half of EU major incidents were attributed to system failure or malfunction, 32% to external events, 19% to process failure and 12% to human error. Cyber security accounted for only 10% [primary]. UK banks told Parliament that their most common causes were third-party suppliers, changes to their own systems, and internal software faults [primary]. Security budgets are aimed at a tenth of the problem.

3. The most expensive gap is between technical detection and business meaning. Institutions see failure quickly and understand its business consequence slowly. The clearest public evidence: during a three-day European retail bank outage in 2025, 56% of online payments failed due to severe degradation of mainframe processing performance, and the bank estimated £5m–£7.5m of compensation for that single incident [primary].

4. Alerting is actively failing as a detection layer. In a February 2026 survey of 1,039 SRE, DevOps and IT operations professionals, 78% reported at least one incident where no alert fired at all, and failure was discovered only after customers were affected. 44% had an outage linked to suppressed or ignored alerts, and 83% of engineers admitted dismissing alerts at least occasionally [vendor]. A separate 2025 observability survey found 73% of organisations had outages linked to ignored alerts [vendor].

5. Regulation has taken control of the response clock. Europe requires an initial filing within 4 hours of classifying an incident as major, and no later than 24 hours after detection [primary]. The US requires notification within 36 hours of determination [primary]. The UK regime lands 18 March 2027 [primary]. Every European classification threshold – share of clients affected, share of transactions, economic impact, duration, geographic spread – is a business question that most institutions cannot answer at speed.

6. The operational safety net is being removed by other regulation. Euro-area instant payments require 10-second settlement, 24/7/365, with only minimal planned maintenance permitted, since October 2025 [primary]. T+1 settlement lands in the UK, EU and Switzerland on 11 October 2027 [primary]. Post-production risk rises structurally even if software quality stays flat.

Cost anchor. Unplanned downtime across the Global 2000 reached an estimated $600bn a year in 2026, a 50% rise in two years, with financial services organisations averaging $309m annually and an average 3.4% share price fall after a single downtime event [vendor, Oxford Economics co-authored]. UK investment banks put the cost of a major outage above £600,000 per hour [vendor survey, n=200+].

PART 1 – The fifteen biggest operational pain points, ranked by business impact

Ranking criterion: business impact, defined as financial exposure plus regulatory exposure plus likelihood of recurrence. Market maturity describes how well the problem is currently served by available products, not how mature the banks are.

1. Cannot translate a technical failure into affected business processes, customers and obligations

Description. Monitoring says a component is degraded. The institution needs to know which payment types, which client segments, which corporate mandates, which regulatory obligations and which settlement windows are affected. That translation is largely manual, performed live, and depends on individuals who know how the estate hangs together.

Business impact. Extends every downstream decision: whether to invoke continuity, whether to notify a supervisor, what to tell corporate clients, whether to compensate. Hours of ambiguity at £600k+ per hour for a large institution.

Customer impact. Customers receive vague or wrong information during the window when accurate information matters most. Corporate clients cannot decide whether to reroute payments.

Technical root causes. No maintained mapping between business processes and the systems that execute them. Service catalogues that describe applications, not business flows. Business logic buried in COBOL, JCL and batch schedules that were never designed to explain themselves. CMDB drift.

Typical owners. COO, Head of Production or Service Management, with operational risk pulled in once reporting is in question.

Market maturity. Low. Observability vendors sell technical topology; process-mining vendors sell design-time analysis. Neither reliably produces a runtime business-impact statement. This is the least-served pain on the list.

2. Cannot classify and file a regulatory incident report inside the regulatory clock

Description. European institutions have 4 hours from classification, and no later than 24 hours from detection, to make an initial filing, then 72 hours for an intermediate report and one month for a final report with root cause. Classification itself depends on quantitative thresholds: more than 10% of clients or transactions affected, economic impact above €100,000, critical services down more than 2 hours, impact across two or more Member States, data losses, criticality of the service.

Business impact. Late or incorrect filing is a sanctionable breach. The defensive response – conservative over-reporting – consumes compliance capacity and invites supervisory attention. Under the UK regime from March 2027 the same pressure arrives with a two-tier reporting structure and an annual register of material third-party arrangements.

Customer impact. Indirect, but supervisory pressure drives the disclosure and redress that customers experience.

Technical root causes. Impact data lives in separate systems from incident data. Transaction-level attribution is a batch reconciliation exercise, not a real-time query. Classification requires a judgement that no single system can evidence.

Typical owners. Operational risk, compliance, CISO, with the CIO accountable for supplying the data.

Market maturity. Medium and crowded on the workflow side, weak on the data side. Many vendors sell the reporting form. Few can populate it.

3. Change-induced production failure

Description. The single largest source of self-inflicted outage. Releases, configuration changes, infrastructure changes and third-party updates land in production and break business flows in ways testing did not predict.

Business impact. Directly implicated in the UK Parliamentary evidence as one of the three most common outage causes [primary]. Drives the rational but costly defensive response: change freezes, extended CAB cycles, slower delivery.

Customer impact. Typically the payday and month-end failures that generate the largest complaint volumes, because change windows cluster around business cycles.

Technical root causes. Impact assessment is a static form filled in by the requesting team. No runtime evidence of what a change actually touches. Insufficient production-like test data. Rollback paths that are theoretical rather than exercised.

Typical owners. Release and change management, platform engineering, application owners.

Market maturity. Medium. Strong CI/CD and feature-flag tooling exists; the gap is business-level blast-radius prediction in regulated estates.

4. Third-party, core-provider and cloud concentration failure

Description. Roughly a third of EU major incidents were attributable to third parties [primary]. Over 40% of cyber incidents reported to the FCA in 2025 involved a third party [primary]. The October 2025 AWS US-EAST-1 event disrupted banking and trading services internationally for around nine hours.

Business impact. The institution retains regulatory accountability for a failure it did not cause and cannot see inside. Structurally worse in the US, where the three largest core providers serve more than 70% of surveyed banks [primary, Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City].

Customer impact. Total loss of service with no credible explanation available from the institution’s own staff.

Technical root causes. No telemetry across the provider boundary. Status pages as the primary detection mechanism. Contractual notification obligations measured in hours.

Typical owners. Vendor and third-party risk, CIO, procurement.

Market maturity. Low to medium. Registers and questionnaires are mature; live operational visibility is not.

5. Alert noise, and failures that generate no alert at all

Description. Two failure modes of the same system. Engineers are saturated with non-actionable alerts, and separately, real failures arrive with no alert.

Business impact. 44% of surveyed organisations had an outage linked to suppressed or ignored alerts; 78% had at least one incident where no alert fired [vendor, n=1,039]. 77% of on-call teams receive at least ten alerts a day and 57% say fewer than 30% are actionable [vendor].

Customer impact. The customer becomes the detection mechanism. First notification arrives via call centre or social media.

Technical root causes. Static thresholds. Tool sprawl with no correlation layer. Alerts written for components rather than for service outcomes. No SLO discipline.

Typical owners. SRE, production support, monitoring or observability teams.

Market maturity. Medium to high. Event correlation and AIOps are commercially mature; adoption and tuning inside banks lag the tooling.

6. Batch and file-level failures discovered after the cut-off

Description. A batch abends, a file is partially processed, or a downstream system silently rejects records. The failure surfaces at reconciliation, after the settlement or payment window has closed. India’s UPI disruptions in late March and April 2025 included bank-side declines attributed to financial year-end closing at the banks, with one April 2025 outage lasting four to five hours – the longest in over three years.

Business impact. Missed cut-offs, settlement fails and penalties, liquidity impact, manual replay and repair effort. Wholesale and commercial banking pain far more than retail.

Customer impact. Corporate clients discover unpaid suppliers, failed payroll or unsettled trades after the fact, when remedy is expensive.

Technical root causes. Batch estates instrumented for job completion, not business completeness. Partial success treated as success. Scheduling dependencies undocumented.

Typical owners. Payments operations, post-trade operations, batch and scheduling teams.

Market maturity. Low. Scheduling tools report job status well and business outcome badly.

7. The mainframe as an observability blind spot

Description. Core banking, card processing and payment engines still run on mainframe platforms that modern observability stacks reach poorly. COBOL-based systems are cited as supporting around 95% of ATM transactions and 80% of in-person card transactions, and roughly 40–43% of core banking systems [secondary, widely cited].

Business impact. The 2025 European bank outage was attributed publicly to severe mainframe processing degradation [primary] – the platform where visibility is weakest is the platform where failure is most consequential.

Customer impact. The longest and most severe consumer-facing outages originate here.

Technical root causes. Separate telemetry, separate tooling, separate skills. Mainframe data rarely joins the same correlation layer as distributed and cloud telemetry.

Typical owners. Mainframe platform teams, largely separate from SRE.

Market maturity. Medium for platform-specific tooling (IBM, Broadcom, BMC), low for integration into a unified operational picture.

8. Disappearance of the maintenance window

Description. Instant payments regulation requires euro-area institutions to send and receive within 10 seconds, 24/7/365, with only minimal planned maintenance [primary]. Real-time rails elsewhere impose the same practical constraint.

Business impact. Removes the overnight buffer that historically absorbed bad changes and allowed file replay. Fraud, sanctions screening and payee verification must clear inside the payment itself.

Customer impact. Fewer planned outages, but a higher proportion of unplanned ones, and no quiet period in which to recover.

Technical root causes. Core platforms architected around batch cycles now serving continuous real-time demand.

Typical owners. Payments engineering, core banking platform owners.

Market maturity. Medium. Zero-downtime deployment patterns are well understood outside banking and poorly transferable into legacy core.

9. Legacy core drag on release safety and operating cost

Description. UK banks were reported to spend around £3.3bn a year – roughly 24% of IT budgets – maintaining legacy systems, with more than half of 150 surveyed banking leaders describing core platforms as a persistent drain [vendor survey].

Business impact. Consumes the budget that would fund resilience improvement, and raises the risk of every change.

Customer impact. Slower product delivery; higher background failure rate.

Technical root causes. Decades of accumulated coupling; shrinking specialist skill pool.

Typical owners. CIO, architecture, core banking platform teams.

Market maturity. High vendor activity in mainframe modernisation, low completion rate. Hybrid strategies dominate; full decommissioning is rare.

10. MTTR inflation from fragmented tooling and manual triage

Description. Time to restore is dominated by establishing facts, not by applying fixes. One published account describes upwards of 100 employees joining triage calls carrying data from separate monitoring tools [vendor]. A 2025 observability survey found 20% of organisations often or always convene a cross-team war room until an issue is resolved [vendor, n=1,855].

Business impact. Direct multiplier on every incident cost. Consumes senior engineering capacity.

Customer impact. Longer outages and slower, less accurate customer communication.

Technical root causes. Siloed telemetry, no shared timeline, escalation by phone tree, undocumented runbooks.

Typical owners. Major incident management, SRE, production support.

Market maturity. Medium to high in tooling; low in realised benefit inside large banks.

11. Root-cause analysis and evidence assembly taking weeks

Description. European rules require a final report with root cause within a month of the last intermediate report [primary]. Assembling a defensible timeline usually means manual reconstruction from logs, tickets and chat history.

Business impact. Compliance effort; delayed remediation; repeat incidents in the interim.

Customer impact. Indirect, via slow remediation of the underlying fault.

Technical root causes. No automatically captured incident timeline. Evidence spread across systems with inconsistent timestamps and retention.

Typical owners. Operational risk, problem management, internal audit.

Market maturity. Low to medium.

12. Recurring incidents that nobody links together

Description. European rules require related recurring incidents to be aggregated when assessing economic impact rather than analysed in isolation [primary]. Most institutions cannot do this reliably.

Business impact. Systematic under-assessment of both cost and regulatory materiality.

Customer impact. The same failure keeps happening to the same customer segment.

Technical root causes. Inconsistent incident categorisation; ticket data unsuitable for pattern analysis.

Typical owners. Problem management.

Market maturity. Low.

13. Capacity and predictable-peak failure

Description. Failures cluster on payday, month-end, financial year-end, tax deadlines and seasonal peaks. The 2025 European bank outage began on payday and coincided with a national tax deadline [primary]. Indian bank-side declines were attributed to financial year-end closing.

Business impact. Maximum customer and regulatory exposure at exactly the predictable moments.

Customer impact. Highest possible – customers are locked out when access matters most.

Technical root causes. Capacity modelling based on averages; batch and online contention; insufficient production-scale load testing.

Typical owners. Capacity management, infrastructure, platform engineering.

Market maturity. Medium. Well-understood discipline, unevenly practised.

14. Disaster recovery and resilience testing that proves compliance, not recovery

Description. European rules require regular resilience testing, and supervisors increasingly want evidence that processes work in practice rather than on paper.

Business impact. Tested recovery capability that does not match real failure modes, discovered during real failure.

Customer impact. Recovery times materially longer than stated impact tolerances.

Technical root causes. Scheduled, scoped, announced tests. Chaos engineering rare in regulated production estates.

Typical owners. Business continuity, resilience, infrastructure.

Market maturity. Medium.

15. SRE and platform skills gap, compounded by mainframe skills scarcity

Description. Banks need two scarce populations at once: modern SRE and platform engineers, and COBOL and mainframe specialists. Reported SRE conditions are poor: around 70% say on-call stress has affected burnout and attrition, median time spent on operations activities rose to 30% in 2025 from 25% in 2024, and 67% report insufficient time for technical training [vendor survey, Catchpoint SRE Report 2025].

Business impact. Attrition in the teams that hold undocumented recovery knowledge.

Customer impact. Indirect, via slower recovery.

Technical root causes. Toil not automated; knowledge not codified.

Typical owners. CIO, engineering leadership, HR.

Market maturity. Low – this is an organisational problem with a partial tooling answer.

PART 2 – Production and SRE challenges across the lifecycle

Thirty-five topics, grouped into seven clusters. Each topic covers: why banks struggle, how widespread the issue is, which institutions are most affected, typical technology stack, existing solutions, remaining gaps.

Cluster A – Incident and response

A1. Incident management

Why banks struggle. Incident processes were designed around ITIL ticket flow and severity codes, not around answering “what is broken for whom”. Severity is assigned before business impact is understood, so escalation and communication run on a guess. Prevalence. Universal. Every institution has a process; few have one that produces a business-impact statement. Most affected. Large universal banks with many product lines and a federated IT estate. Typical stack. ServiceNow or BMC Helix for the record, Teams/Slack/Zoom bridges for coordination, PagerDuty or xMatters for paging. Existing solutions. Mature workflow and on-call tooling. Remaining gap. The ticket knows the application. It does not know the business process, the client segment, or the regulatory obligation.

A2. Major incident response

Why banks struggle. Severity-one response is a coordination exercise before it is a technical one. One published account describes upwards of 100 employees joining triage calls carrying data from separate monitoring tools [vendor]. A 2025 observability survey found 20% of organisations often or always convene a cross-team war room until resolution [vendor, n=1,855]. Prevalence. High, and worse in institutions with strong functional silos. Most affected. Tier-one banks; institutions with heavy outsourcing where key knowledge sits with suppliers. Typical stack. Bridge call plus incident channel plus a manually maintained timeline. Existing solutions. Incident command tooling, comms automation, status pages. Remaining gap. No shared, automatically assembled evidence picture. Facts are established verbally and re-established for each new joiner.

A3. MTTR reduction

Why banks struggle. MTTR is dominated by time to identify and time to understand, not time to fix. Cross-industry benchmarks put elite failed-deployment recovery under one hour and low performers at one week to one month [secondary, DORA metrics]. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Institutions with fragmented telemetry across mainframe, distributed and cloud. Typical stack. Multiple observability tools by domain, rarely correlated. Existing solutions. Event correlation and AIOps claim 40–58% MTTR reduction in published cases [vendor]. Remaining gap. Reductions are demonstrated on distributed estates. The mainframe and batch portions of MTTR are largely untouched.

A4. Production stability

Why banks struggle. Stability is managed as an aggregate availability number, which hides the pattern that matters: failures cluster on payday, month-end, financial year-end and tax deadlines. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Retail banks with concentrated salary-cycle load. Typical stack. Availability reporting from monitoring plus incident counts. Existing solutions. SLO frameworks where adopted. Remaining gap. Aggregate uptime is a poor proxy for customer-experienced stability at peaks.

A5. Availability

Why banks struggle. Availability is measured per component, while regulators and customers experience it per service. UK operational resilience already requires important business services and impact tolerances; the incident and third-party reporting regime lands 18 March 2027 [primary]. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. UK and EU institutions, where service-level definition is now a supervisory expectation. Typical stack. Synthetic monitoring, uptime dashboards, CMDB. Existing solutions. Business service mapping products. Remaining gap. Maps are built once and decay. There is little runtime verification that the map is still true.

A6. Reliability

Why banks struggle. Reliability engineering asks for error budgets and controlled risk-taking. Banking governance asks for zero defects. The two operating models conflict, and the bank usually resolves it by slowing change. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Traditional institutions; digital-only banks are markedly better here. Typical stack. SLO tooling (Nobl9, native observability SLOs) where present. Existing solutions. Well-documented practice from outside banking. Remaining gap. Error budgets are hard to reconcile with impact tolerances that regulators treat as commitments.

Cluster B – Change, release and delivery

B1. Change management

Why banks struggle. Impact assessment is a form completed by the requesting team from memory. UK banks named changes to their own systems among the three most common causes of outage [primary]. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. Institutions with large change volumes across coupled legacy estates. Typical stack. ServiceNow Change, CAB governance, freeze calendars. Existing solutions. Change risk scoring, standard-change automation. Remaining gap. No runtime evidence of blast radius. The CAB approves a description of a change, not its actual reach.

B2. Release management

Why banks struggle. Release trains are coordinated across dozens of teams and a core platform that cannot deploy on demand. The pace is set by the slowest, most fragile component. Prevalence. High in traditional banks. Most affected. Banks running quarterly or monthly core releases alongside continuous digital delivery. Typical stack. Release calendars, Jenkins/GitLab/Azure DevOps, manual gates. Existing solutions. Release orchestration tooling. Remaining gap. Two-speed IT is a structural mismatch, not a tooling problem.

B3. Deployment risk

Why banks struggle. Cross-industry change failure rates put top performers at 0–15% and low performers at 45–60% [secondary, DORA metrics]. Regulated estates with infrequent large releases sit at the wrong end because batch size is large. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Core banking and payments platforms. Typical stack. Blue-green and canary where the architecture allows; big-bang where it does not. Existing solutions. Progressive delivery, feature flags (LaunchDarkly, Harness). Remaining gap. Progressive delivery assumes you can route a fraction of traffic. Much of core banking cannot.

B4. Rollback

Why banks struggle. Rollback is straightforward for stateless services and very hard once a change has written to a ledger, altered a schema, or been consumed downstream by a settlement process. Prevalence. High for anything touching data. Most affected. Core banking, payments, post-trade. Typical stack. Database migration tooling, backup and restore, compensating transactions. Existing solutions. Fix-forward discipline; database versioning tools. Remaining gap. Rollback plans are documented but rarely exercised under production conditions.

B5. CI/CD in regulated environments

Why banks struggle. Segregation of duties, evidence retention, approval gates and audit trails are frequently implemented as manual steps bolted onto an automated pipeline, which reintroduces the delay automation removed. Prevalence. Universal in regulated institutions. Most affected. Institutions under multiple regimes simultaneously. Typical stack. GitLab, Azure DevOps, Jenkins, Harness, plus manual sign-off. Existing solutions. Policy-as-code, automated evidence capture. Remaining gap. Auditors accept documents more readily than pipeline attestations, so the manual step survives.

B6. DevSecOps

Why banks struggle. Security testing is the most successful shift-left story in banking and also the one that most often stops at the pre-production boundary. Runtime security context rarely reaches the incident bridge. Prevalence. High adoption, uneven depth. Most affected. All. Typical stack. SAST/DAST/SCA in pipeline, CSPM in cloud. Existing solutions. Mature and consolidating. Remaining gap. Cyber accounts for only about 10% of EU major incidents [primary], yet attracts a disproportionate share of assurance investment relative to change and process failure.

Cluster C – Detection and diagnosis

C1. Observability

Why banks struggle. Observability is deployed per domain and per team, producing three or four parallel truths. Cost control then caps retention and cardinality, removing exactly the detail needed during a rare, severe incident. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. Large estates with mainframe plus distributed plus multi-cloud. Typical stack. Datadog, Dynatrace, Splunk, Elastic, Grafana, New Relic, OpenTelemetry. Existing solutions. Very mature for cloud-native workloads. Remaining gap. Business-process observability. The tools answer “is the service healthy”, not “did the payment run complete correctly”.

C2. Monitoring

Why banks struggle. Legacy monitoring is threshold-based and component-scoped, generating alerts that describe symptoms without consequence. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. Institutions with long-lived monitoring estates. Typical stack. Legacy platform monitoring alongside modern observability. Existing solutions. Consolidation programmes, OpenTelemetry standardisation. Remaining gap. Consolidation is a multi-year programme with weak business sponsorship.

C3. Alert fatigue

Why banks struggle. 77% of on-call teams receive at least ten alerts a day and 57% say fewer than 30% are actionable; 83% of engineers admit dismissing alerts at least occasionally [vendor, n=1,039]. A 2025 survey found 73% of organisations had outages linked to ignored alerts [vendor]. Prevalence. Very high. Most affected. Everyone; worse where tool sprawl is greatest. Typical stack. PagerDuty, Opsgenie, native alerting. Existing solutions. Noise reduction, correlation, SLO-based alerting. Remaining gap. The inverse failure is under-served: 78% reported at least one incident where no alert fired at all [vendor].

C4. Event correlation

Why banks struggle. Correlation requires a shared model of how things depend on each other. Most banks do not have one that is accurate at runtime. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Complex estates. Typical stack. BigPanda, Moogsoft, ScienceLogic, Dynatrace Davis, Splunk ITSI. Existing solutions. Commercially mature. Remaining gap. Correlation groups technical events into a technical story. Nobody converts that story into a business one.

C5. Root cause analysis

Why banks struggle. European rules require a final report with root cause within one month of the last intermediate report [primary], and recurring related incidents must be aggregated rather than analysed in isolation [primary]. Evidence assembly is manual. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. EU and UK institutions under formal reporting duty. Typical stack. Log search, ticket history, chat archaeology. Existing solutions. Post-incident review tooling (Blameless, incident.io, Jeli-style products). Remaining gap. Automatic timeline capture with regulatory-grade evidence integrity.

Cluster D – Performance, capacity and data

D1. Capacity management

Why banks struggle. Capacity is modelled on averages while failures occur at predictable peaks. The 2025 European bank outage began on payday and coincided with a national tax deadline [primary]. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Retail banks and real-time payment participants. Typical stack. Capacity planning tools, mainframe MIPS forecasting, cloud autoscaling. Existing solutions. Elastic scaling in cloud. Remaining gap. The constrained tier is usually the one that cannot scale elastically – core and database.

D2. Performance degradation

Why banks struggle. Partial degradation is harder than outage. Systems stay “up” while success rates fall. Indian real-time payments show this clearly: intermittent declines and latency increases at specific banks rather than a clean outage. Prevalence. Very high, and under-reported. Most affected. Real-time payment participants. Typical stack. APM plus synthetic transactions. Existing solutions. Success-rate monitoring where implemented. Remaining gap. Degradation rarely triggers formal incident process until customers complain.

D3. Database bottlenecks

Why banks struggle. The database is the least elastic component and the most shared. Lock contention between batch and online workloads is a recurring cause of peak-time degradation. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Institutions running batch and online against the same core data store. Typical stack. Db2 on z/OS, Oracle, SQL Server, increasingly PostgreSQL for new services. Existing solutions. Database performance tooling, read replicas, CQRS patterns for new builds. Remaining gap. Cannot be fixed without re-architecting the core.

D4. Batch failures

Why banks struggle. Batch is instrumented for job completion, not business completeness. A job can succeed while silently dropping records. Indian bank-side transaction declines during the March–April 2025 UPI disruptions were attributed by NPCI to financial year-end closing at banks. Prevalence. High. Most affected. Wholesale, payments and post-trade operations. Typical stack. Control-M, IBM Workload Scheduler, Autosys, JCL. Existing solutions. Scheduling tools with dependency management. Remaining gap. Business-outcome verification of batch – did the right number of the right records reach the right place.

Cluster E – Platform and estate

E1. Mainframe integration

Why banks struggle. Separate telemetry, separate tooling, separate teams. COBOL-based systems are cited as supporting around 95% of ATM transactions and 80% of in-person card transactions [secondary]. Prevalence. High among established institutions. Most affected. Tier-one banks, card processors, European incumbents running core in-house. Typical stack. z/OS, CICS, Db2, IMS, MQ; IBM Z Operations, Broadcom, BMC AMI. Existing solutions. Platform-specific observability is capable. Remaining gap. Joining mainframe signals to the distributed picture in one correlated view. This is where the most consequential outages originate and where visibility is weakest.

E2. Legacy systems

Why banks struggle. UK banks were reported to spend around £3.3bn a year, roughly 24% of IT budgets, maintaining legacy [vendor]. That spend crowds out resilience investment. Prevalence. Universal outside digital-only banks. Most affected. Established retail and universal banks. Typical stack. Decades of accumulated coupling. Existing solutions. Modernisation programmes; hybrid strategies dominate. Remaining gap. Very few complete. The interim state – part legacy, part modern – is often less observable than either.

E3. Hybrid cloud

Why banks struggle. The hybrid boundary is where transactions lose traceability. A payment crosses from a cloud-native channel through middleware into a mainframe core and back, with a different telemetry regime at each hop. Prevalence. The default architecture for large banks. Most affected. Institutions mid-migration. Typical stack. AWS/Azure/GCP plus on-premises core, connected by MQ, Kafka or API gateways. Existing solutions. Distributed tracing where instrumentation is complete. Remaining gap. Trace continuity across the legacy boundary is rarely achieved.

E4. Multi-cloud

Why banks struggle. Adopted for concentration risk and regulatory pressure, at the cost of duplicated operating models. The October 2025 AWS US-EAST-1 event affected banking and trading services for around nine hours and renewed supervisory focus on concentration risk. Prevalence. Growing, driven by regulation more than engineering preference. Most affected. EU institutions under third-party oversight rules; UK firms facing the March 2027 register requirement [primary]. Typical stack. Two hyperscalers plus abstraction tooling. Existing solutions. Infrastructure-as-code, Kubernetes as a portability layer. Remaining gap. True workload portability for stateful financial workloads remains largely theoretical.

E5. Kubernetes

Why banks struggle. Kubernetes moves complexity rather than removing it. Platform teams become a new critical dependency, and cluster-level failures have wide blast radius. Prevalence. High for new build, low for core. Most affected. Banks building digital channels and API layers. Typical stack. OpenShift, EKS, AKS, GKE. Existing solutions. Managed control planes, service mesh, GitOps. Remaining gap. Skills concentration and configuration drift; incidents caused by platform changes rather than application changes.

E6. API failures

Why banks struggle. Open banking and API banking expose internal fragility to external parties who monitor and publish availability. An API responds successfully while returning stale or incomplete data – a failure mode conventional monitoring passes. Prevalence. High and growing. Most affected. EU and UK banks under open banking obligations; API-banking providers to corporates. Typical stack. API gateways, developer portals, mandated performance reporting. Existing solutions. Gateway analytics, synthetic API monitoring. Remaining gap. Semantic correctness, not just availability and latency.

E7. Microservices complexity

Why banks struggle. Service count grows faster than the ability to reason about dependencies. Each new service is a new failure mode and a new on-call surface. Prevalence. High in digital estates. Most affected. Banks that decomposed the channel layer without decomposing the core. Typical stack. Containers, service mesh, event streaming. Existing solutions. Distributed tracing, service catalogues. Remaining gap. Catalogues describe ownership, not business meaning.

E8. Middleware and integration layers

Why banks struggle. The integration tier carries the actual business flow and is usually the least owned. Queues, adapters and file transfers sit between teams. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. Institutions with layered legacy integration. Typical stack. IBM MQ, Kafka, ESB products, MFT. Existing solutions. Queue-depth monitoring. Remaining gap. Queue depth is a technical symptom. Nobody translates it into affected business transactions in real time.

Cluster F – Assurance and resilience

F1. Production testing

Why banks struggle. Test environments are not production-shaped, and production-like data is restricted for privacy reasons. Defects that only appear at production scale and data variety survive to release. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. Institutions with complex data privacy constraints. Typical stack. Test data management, service virtualisation. Existing solutions. Synthetic data generation, contract testing. Remaining gap. Fidelity gap is structural.

F2. Chaos engineering

Why banks struggle. Deliberate failure injection into a regulated production estate is culturally and contractually hard. Adoption in banking has largely shifted left into pre-production rather than production. Prevalence. Growing. One survey cited 59% of organisations deploying chaos engineering, with MTTR improvement the most cited benefit [secondary, Gartner Peer Community via vendor summary]. Most affected. Banks with cloud-native estates adopt fastest. Typical stack. Gremlin, LitmusChaos, Harness Chaos, AWS FIS. Existing solutions. Mature tooling. Remaining gap. Almost never applied to the core, which is where the severe incidents originate.

F3. Disaster recovery

Why banks struggle. DR is designed for site loss and exercised on schedule, while real incidents are partial, gradual degradations that no runbook matches. Prevalence. Universal, well governed, weakly predictive. Most affected. All regulated institutions. Typical stack. Active-passive data centres, increasingly active-active for digital tiers. Existing solutions. Recovery orchestration tooling. Remaining gap. Evidence that recovery works under realistic, unannounced conditions.

F4. Resilience

Why banks struggle. DORA (regulation) Article 24 sets a framework for digital operational resilience testing that asks whether controls demonstrably work, not whether tools were purchased. Supervisors in 2026 increasingly want evidence of practice, not policy. Prevalence. Universal in the EU; UK equivalent lands March 2027 [primary]. Most affected. EU entities and their service providers. Typical stack. Resilience testing programmes, threat-led penetration testing. Existing solutions. Governance frameworks. Remaining gap. The distance between a documented impact tolerance and a demonstrated recovery time.

F5. Compliance impact on operations

Why banks struggle. Compliance requirements now dictate operational tempo: 4-hour European filing clocks, 36-hour US notification, 24/7/365 instant payments, and no meaningful maintenance window [primary]. Prevalence. Universal. Most affected. EU and UK institutions. Typical stack. GRC platforms disconnected from operational telemetry. Existing solutions. Regulatory reporting workflow products. Remaining gap. The data needed to complete the filing sits in operations; the obligation sits in compliance; nothing joins them.

Cluster G – Operating model

G1. AIOps

Why banks struggle. Adoption is real but shallow – automated correlation is accepted, automated remediation in production is not, for good governance reasons. Prevalence. Growing fast. The AIOps market for financial services is estimated at $5.03bn in 2025 rising to $6.36bn in 2026, a 26.4% CAGR, and projected to reach $16.12bn by 2030 [vendor market report]. Most affected. All large institutions are buying. Typical stack. Dynatrace, Splunk, BigPanda, Moogsoft, ServiceNow AIOps. Existing solutions. Anomaly detection and correlation are commercially proven. Remaining gap. Explainability and auditability. A bank must be able to tell a supervisor why an automated action was taken.

G2. SRE adoption maturity

Why banks struggle. SRE is frequently adopted as a job title rather than an operating model – production support renamed, without error budgets, without engineering time protected. Reported conditions are poor: around 70% say on-call stress has affected burnout and attrition, median time on operations activities rose to 30% in 2025 from 25% in 2024, and 67% report insufficient time for technical training [vendor, Catchpoint SRE Report 2025]. Prevalence. Widespread title adoption, limited practice adoption. Most affected. Traditional banks; digital-only banks are genuinely ahead. Typical stack. SLO tooling, incident platforms, internal developer platforms. Existing solutions. Well-documented practice. Remaining gap. Error budgets conflict with zero-defect governance culture, so the discipline that makes SRE work is the first thing dropped.

G3. Platform engineering

Why banks struggle. Internal developer platforms succeed for greenfield digital work and stop at the legacy boundary, so the platform serves the minority of the estate that was already easiest. Prevalence. High investment, uneven return. Most affected. Large banks with strong engineering functions. Typical stack. Backstage, OpenShift, GitOps toolchains. Existing solutions. Maturing rapidly. Remaining gap. Golden paths do not extend to the core, which is where risk concentrates.

G4. AI-assisted delivery – an emerging risk

DORA (metrics) research in 2025 found that increased AI adoption correlates with increased software delivery instability, even as it improves individual effectiveness, most plausibly because code generation outpaces the capacity of review and deployment infrastructure to absorb it [secondary]. For banks this points one way: change volume rises while change assurance stays flat. Every pain in Cluster B gets worse before it gets better.

PART 3 – Customer pain

A. Retail and consumer banking

A1. Mobile app failure and login failure

Complaints. Cannot log in, app spins, session drops mid-transaction, balance does not load. Customers do not distinguish app failure from core failure – they experience both as “the bank is down”. Financial impact. Call centre surge, redress, and in the UK measurable compensation. One European bank estimated £5m–£7.5m for a single three-day outage [primary]. Reputation impact. Highest of any failure class, because it is public, simultaneous and visible on outage trackers and social media within minutes. Frequency. The most frequent customer-visible failure category. Real examples. Barclays, 31 January to 2 February 2025, roughly three days, beginning on payday and coinciding with the UK self-assessment tax deadline [primary]. Lloyds, Halifax, Bank of Scotland, TSB, Nationwide and First Direct app failures on 28 February 2025 [primary reporting]. During the AWS US-EAST-1 outage of 20 October 2025, Lloyds Banking Group and Bank of Scotland were among the affected, alongside Robinhood, Coinbase, Venmo and Chime.

A2. Payment failures and slow transfers

Complaints. Payment accepted then reversed, transfer pending for hours, recipient not credited, duplicate debit. Financial impact. Direct redress; in the UK, consequential loss claims. The Barclays incident saw 56% of online payments fail, attributed to severe degradation of mainframe processing performance [primary], and reported consequences included stalled mortgage transactions. Reputation impact. Severe, because consequences are irreversible in the customer’s life – a missed completion, an unpaid bill, a failed rent payment. Frequency. High, and concentrated at month-end and payday. Real examples. India’s UPI disruptions of 26 March, 2 April and 12 April 2025; the April incident ran four to five hours, the longest in over three years, attributed to API overload from multiple banks. NPCI attributed a separate episode to financial year-end closing at banks. Nigeria’s NIBSS instant payment platform suffered downtime in May 2026, disrupting transfers across banks and fintechs.

A3. Card declines and authorisation failure

Complaints. Card declined at point of sale with funds available; declined online; contactless not working. Financial impact. Interchange revenue loss, merchant relationship damage, immediate customer attrition risk. Reputation impact. High and disproportionate – a decline is a public humiliation in a queue. Frequency. Lower than app failure, higher severity per event. Real examples. COBOL-based systems are cited as supporting roughly 95% of ATM transactions and 80% of in-person card transactions [secondary] – authorisation failures usually originate deep in the stack.

A4. Delayed deposits and credited-but-not-visible balances

Complaints. Salary not showing, cheque not cleared, balance wrong. Financial impact. Overdraft fees wrongly applied, subsequent remediation and refund exercises. Reputation impact. Severe when it coincides with payday. Frequency. Moderate; almost always batch-related. Real examples. The Barclays incident produced inaccurate account balances persisting for days [primary reporting]. Nigeria’s NIP platform produced a “dry posting” fault in September 2024 in which beneficiary accounts were credited without corresponding debits from originating accounts, an error reported at ₦13.66bn.

A5. Fraud alerts and false positives

Complaints. Legitimate transaction blocked, card frozen while travelling, payment held for verification. Financial impact. Abandoned transactions, call centre load, attrition. Reputation impact. Moderate but cumulative – erodes trust in the product’s reliability. Frequency. Very high, and rising with instant payments. Structural cause. Instant payment rules require full sanctions, fraud and payee verification checks inside a 10-second window [primary]. Compressing the check increases both false positives and the cost of getting it wrong.

A6. Downtime and poor digital experience generally

Complaints. Service unavailable, degraded performance, inconsistent behaviour across channels. Financial impact. UK investment banks put a major outage above £600,000 per hour [vendor, n=200+]. Financial services organisations average $309m in annual downtime cost [vendor]. Reputation impact. An average 3.4% share price fall after a single downtime event across the Global 2000 [vendor]. Frequency. Nine UK banks recorded 158 IT failures and 803 hours of unplanned outage over two years [primary].

B. Enterprise and commercial banking

B1. Treasury services and cash management

Complaints. Cannot see intraday position, balance reporting late or incomplete, sweep did not execute. Financial impact. Funding decisions made on stale data; overdraft and funding cost; failed intraday liquidity management. Reputation impact. High and durable – corporate treasurers move mandates. Frequency. Moderate; high impact per event. Detection difficulty. Very high. Reporting can be silently incomplete rather than absent.

B2. Payments and SWIFT

Complaints. Payment not sent before cut-off, message rejected, status unknown, duplicate instruction. Financial impact. Late payment penalties, FX exposure, counterparty and correspondent friction. Reputation impact. Severe. Corporate clients measure banks on payment reliability above almost everything else. Frequency. Moderate; almost always associated with batch and cut-off windows. Structural cause. Batch estates instrumented for job completion, not business completeness – a run can succeed while dropping records.

B3. Real-time payments and settlement

Complaints. Instant payment not instant; settlement fail; reconciliation break. Financial impact. Settlement fails carry penalties. T+1 settlement lands in the UK, EU and Switzerland on 11 October 2027, with allocations and confirmations required on trade date and a double settlement day expected on 12 October 2027 [primary]. Reputation impact. High in wholesale markets, where operational performance is a selection criterion. Frequency. Rising as instant rails become mandatory.

B4. Corporate portals, API banking and open banking

Complaints. Portal slow or unavailable; API returning stale, partial or inconsistent data; certificate and onboarding failures. Financial impact. Corporate clients cannot execute; third-party providers publish availability statistics. Reputation impact. Uniquely exposed – open banking obligations make performance externally visible. Frequency. High. Structural cause. An API can return HTTP 200 with wrong or stale content. Availability monitoring passes; the customer is still broken.

B5. Trade finance

Complaints. Document processing delays, guarantee issuance late, manual intervention required. Financial impact. Shipment and working capital delays for the client. Reputation impact. Moderate; relationship-mediated. Frequency. Lower, but recovery is slow because processes are semi-manual.

B6. Payroll and bulk file processing

Complaints. Payroll file partially processed; employees unpaid; no clear status. Financial impact. Employer-level crisis, immediate escalation to relationship management, compensation exposure. Reputation impact. Extremely high – it becomes the client’s employees’ problem. Frequency. Low but catastrophic; concentrated at month-end.

B7. Liquidity management

Complaints. Position data late or wrong; cannot fund accurately. Financial impact. Direct funding cost. Instant payments introduce 24/7 liquidity requirements, and around half of surveyed banks anticipated losing interest earnings to the new demands [vendor]. Reputation impact. Internal rather than external, but it drives CFO attention. Frequency. Continuous pressure rather than discrete incidents.

The two are usually different places, and the distance between them is the diagnostic problem.

The three patterns worth naming

1. Incidents originate deep and surface shallow. The customer-visible symptom is almost always in the channel. The cause is frequently several layers below, in core processing, integration or a provider. Institutions instrument the channel best and the core worst, which is precisely backwards relative to severity.

2. Detection difficulty is inversely correlated with observability investment. Cloud-native services are the easiest to diagnose and receive the most modern tooling. The mainframe and the provider boundary are the hardest and receive the least. The 2025 European bank outage attributed publicly to severe mainframe processing degradation [primary] is the archetype.

3. Digital channels are the alarm, not the fault. Channel teams absorb the blame and the escalation because they hold the customer relationship. This distorts prioritisation: investment follows where the complaint arrives rather than where the failure begins.

Implication

If you were designing a product for this market, the highest-value position is not another channel monitor. It is whatever can trace a channel symptom back to a core or integration cause, expressed in business terms, faster than a bridge call can. Every layer above already has adequate tooling. The two layers with “very high” detection difficulty – the core and the provider boundary – are where the severe incidents live and where the market is thinnest.

Two observations for anyone selling into this market

The budget follows the deadline. In Europe and the UK, resilience and reporting spend has a named owner, a board reporting line and an externally imposed date. Nowhere else in this list does. That makes EU and UK institutions the highest-probability first buyers for anything framed around incident classification, impact quantification and evidence.

The regions with the most pain are not the regions with the most budget. Africa and parts of Asia-Pacific show the most acute post-production failure and the least capacity to pay for sophisticated tooling. Middle East institutions have budget and executive sponsorship but less accumulated legacy pain. The intersection of high pain, high budget and a regulatory deadline sits in Europe and the UK.

What the landscape adds up to

Every layer of the technical stack is served, most of them well. Three things are not served by anyone on this list:

  1. The translation from technical fault to business process, customer and obligation. Correlation vendors group technical events. ITSM vendors record them. Nobody produces the business-impact statement.
  2. Regulatory-grade impact quantification. No vendor on this list can populate the client, transaction and economic impact fields a European filing requires.
  3. Business-outcome verification of batch and file processing. Schedulers report job status. Nobody verifies that the right records reached the right place.

PART 9 – Market gaps

Problems that remain largely unsolved

  1. Runtime business-process to system mapping. Every bank has a stale spreadsheet or CMDB view. None has a live, verified one. This single absence blocks nine of the eleven internal teams in Part 4.
  2. Business-impact quantification during an incident. Which customers, which transactions, what value, which obligations – assembled manually, under time pressure, from tribal knowledge.
  3. Regulatory classification support. European classification thresholds are quantitative [primary], and no product populates them from operational data.
  4. Silent failure detection. 78% of surveyed teams had at least one incident where no alert fired at all [vendor]. The market sells noise reduction; the inverse failure is barely addressed.
  5. Batch and file business-outcome verification. Partial success treated as success.
  6. Cross-boundary tracing into third parties and core providers. Structurally worse in the US, where most institutions do not own the core.
  7. Mainframe-to-distributed correlation in a single operational picture. Dynatrace and IBM come closest; nobody has solved it end to end.
  8. Incident evidence with regulatory integrity. Timelines are reconstructed manually weeks later.
  9. Linking recurring related incidents. Required in Europe for aggregate materiality [primary], unavailable in practice.
  10. Pre-release business blast-radius prediction. CAB approves a description, not a measured reach.

Where banks still rely on manual processes

  • Impact assessment on change requests – self-declared, from memory
  • Business-impact determination during major incidents – bridge call, verbally
  • Regulatory classification and filing – spreadsheets under a four-hour clock
  • Post-incident timeline construction – log and chat archaeology
  • Batch failure triage and replay – operations staff, next morning
  • Third-party incident status – status pages and phone calls
  • Reconciliation break investigation – manual, at scale, daily

Operational bottlenecks

The recurring shape: detection is fast, comprehension is slow, and every downstream decision waits on comprehension. Escalation, continuity invocation, supervisory notification, customer communication and compensation all queue behind a question no system answers.

Opportunities for AI

  • Inferring business-process structure from transaction and log data rather than requiring manual mapping – the highest-value AI application in this space
  • Generating a first-draft business-impact statement within minutes of detection
  • Drafting regulatory filings with populated quantitative fields and an evidence trail
  • Detecting absence – the transaction that should have happened and did not
  • Automatic incident timeline assembly with provenance
  • Linking recurring incidents by behavioural similarity rather than ticket category

The constraint on all of these: a bank must be able to explain to a supervisor why an automated conclusion was reached. Explainability is not a feature here, it is the entry requirement.

Opportunities for automation

  • Automated evidence capture for change approval and post-incident review
  • Automated impact-scoped customer communication
  • Runbook execution with audit trail, short of full auto-remediation
  • Batch completeness checks as a standard control rather than a bespoke script

Opportunities for new products

The gap with the clearest shape: a business-impact layer that sits above existing observability and ITSM, consumes their signals, and answers “what broke, for whom, what does it cost, and what must we declare”. It does not compete with Datadog or ServiceNow. It consumes both.

Closing assessment

The evidence supports the central proposition. Post-production is where banking’s operational cost, customer harm and regulatory exposure concentrate, and the specific unserved gap is the translation from technical fault to business meaning. That gap is unusually well-defended as a market position: the observability majors are optimised for technical depth, the ITSM majors for process record, and neither is structurally motivated to solve the join between them.

Three caveats belong in any investment conversation.

First, the incumbents are close. Dynatrace in particular already has causal AI and genuine mainframe coverage. It is a product decision away from this space, and it already sits inside these institutions.

Second, the data problem may be a services problem. Deriving business-process meaning from systems that were never designed to explain themselves is the whole technical bet. If it requires months of bespoke work per institution, this is a consultancy, not a product.

Third, the sales cycle is brutal. Production access in a regulated bank means security review, model risk assessment, procurement and change approval. Twelve to eighteen months is normal, and the buyer with the deadline is in Europe and the UK, not everywhere.

The validation questions in the accompanying presentation are designed to test the second and third of these, which are the ones that decide the outcome.

Regional evidence

Three markets prove the thesis in public

India — partial degradationUPI disruptions on 26 March, 2 April and 12 April 2025. The April incident ran 4–5 hours, the longest in over three years, triggered by API overload from multiple banks. A separate episode was attributed by NPCI to financial year-end closing at the banks.

The failure is not an outage of the rail — it is a drop in success rates at specific banks, which availability monitoring passes.

Split 3

Consumer vs business — different pain, different buyer

Consumer (B2C)
  • Failure looks like: app down on payday, card declined, balance wrong, payment stuck
  • Clock: minutes. Social media sets the narrative before the incident bridge does
  • Cost: redress and compensation. One European bank faced £5m–£7.5m for a single three-day outage
  • Owner: COO / Head of Retail Operations
  • Metric that matters: time to tell the customer something true
Business (B2B / wholesale)
  • Failure looks like: a payment file not processed, a settlement missed, a report filed late
  • Clock: cut-off times — often discovered hours later, once the window has closed
  • Cost: SLA penalties, settlement fails, liquidity cost, a corporate client who moves the mandate
  • Owner: Head of Operations / Payments / Post-trade
  • Metric that matters: which transactions were affected, exactly — down to the reference
Consumer sells on speed of explanation. Business sells on precision of scope. Same capability, two different pitches and two different proof points.

Counter-case

What would kill this thesis

1
Incumbents move first

Dynatrace already has causal AI and genuine mainframe coverage. It is a product decision away from this space, and already inside these banks.

2
The data problem is a services problem

Business-process meaning has to be derived from systems never designed to explain themselves. If that needs months of bespoke work per bank, this is a consultancy, not a product.

3
The sales cycle outruns the runway

Production access in a regulated bank means security review, model risk, procurement and change approval. Twelve to eighteen months is normal.

4
Banks build it themselves

Large institutions have data platforms and mainframe expertise, and may treat this as an internal programme.

5
Compliance tooling gets there first

A crowded set of vendors already sells regulatory incident reporting — weaker product, easier story.

6
AI raises change volume faster than assurance

2025 research links higher AI adoption to higher delivery instability. More pain, but also more noise to sell against.

Risks 1 and 3 together are the fatal combination — which is why the validation calls should test speed of proof, not interest in the idea.
